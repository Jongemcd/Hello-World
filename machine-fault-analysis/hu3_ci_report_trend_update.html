HU3 Continuous Improvement Report: Trend Update

HU3's fault log refreshed through Sep 15, 2026 — about two more months beyond the last report (which ended Jul 16). Executive summary

Across the full Mar 16 – Sep 15, 2026 window, HU3 logged 18,900 stoppage episodes totaling 1,361.3 hours, of which 766.6 hours (56.3%) trace to a confirmed technical root cause — essentially unchanged from the 55.5% reported in July. The true-first-fault correction from the last report holds up perfectly on the new data: "main air valve pressure not present" is still the true first fault in zero of 11,493 occurrences.

A naive month-over-month comparison first suggested a dramatic, across-the-board improvement — but that turned out to be mostly an artifact of two long stretches with no logged data at all, which made the recent period look far quieter than it really was per hour of actual operation. A second, independent data source (EPICOR MI, the plant's separate downtime-tracking system) confirms those stretches were not a shutdown — it shows normal fault activity throughout both gaps — so this is a genuine Ignition logging outage, not idle time, and its own gap-free data independently corroborates the corrected conclusion below rather than the naive one. The honest picture: one confirmed, durable win (guard/interlock stoppages down 94%), most causes essentially flat, and eight statistically real watch-outs — most notably a fast-growing vacuum counter-plate sensing issue and a new stroke-limiter fault that was negligible in the spring and is now a recurring problem.

A confound worth flagging: why the first comparison was misleading

The obvious way to check for a trend is: count events per calendar day in the old period, count events per calendar day in the new period, compare. Doing that here made it look like almost everything had improved dramatically. But two enormous gaps in the log — 474 hours (19.75 days) starting Aug 27, and 376 hours (15.6 days) starting Sep 12 — meant roughly 850 of the ~1,478 hours in the "recent" window have no logged rows at all. Counting those silent hours as normal calendar time makes every rate look artificially low, because the denominator includes hours where nothing could possibly have happened. (The next section checks what actually caused those silent hours — the answer isn't what a first guess would suggest.)

The fix, in plain English: instead of "events per calendar day," this report uses "events per hour the machine was actually logged as running or stopped" — i.e., hours we know something was really happening, gaps excluded. Once that's corrected, the two periods turn out to have been about equally busy per hour of real activity (46.0% of logged time down in the baseline period vs. 48.0% in the recent one) — so the dramatic "improvement" mostly disappears, and a more honest, smaller set of real changes remains.

Cross-system validation: what EPICOR MI says about the same two months

Two files landed after the last report: HU3's downtime events and hours from EPICOR MI — a separate plant system that tracks downtime independently of the Ignition/PLC historian everything else in this report is built from, using its own reason taxonomy (42 categories like "Electrical," "Valve Feed," "No Demand," "Safety") logged as a daily total rather than a row-level change log. Different system, different categories, same machine, almost the same date range (Mar 16 – Sep 22). That makes it a genuine independent check, not a repeat of the same measurement — worth asking two things: does it explain the Ignition gaps, and does it agree with the corrected trend conclusion above?

It resolves the gap: this was a logging outage, not a shutdown

EPICOR has a value for all 191 days in range — no gaps. On the 44 days where Ignition logs zero downtime (including both of the long August/September stretches), EPICOR shows 451.8 hours across 6,149 downtime events — normal, continuous fault activity, not a quiet line. Only one day, Jun 28, is genuinely silent in both systems — that one's a real shutdown or holiday. The other 43 days, including the two big blocks: HU3 was running and faulting the whole time; Ignition's historian simply wasn't capturing it. That changes what "fix the logging pipeline" in action item 7 below is asking for — not a scheduling question, an actual data-capture defect.

Why this matters beyond just explaining the gap: because Ignition has zero rows for those 44 days, every root-cause figure in this report (the Pareto, the watch-outs, the 8.49→0.49 interlock number) is silently missing whatever happened on them. The trend conclusions aren't wrong, but they're built on an incomplete recent period — real fault activity from 44 days is invisible to the fault-code-level analysis, even though we now know it happened.

It independently confirms the "not broadly improving" correction

EPICOR has no gaps, so its own baseline-vs-recent comparison needs no exposure correction — a useful second opinion using a completely different method. Tested the same way (exact binomial test, Bonferroni-corrected across 20 categories with enough volume): EPICOR's largest categories mostly got worse, not better — the same direction this report's corrected Ignition analysis found, independently arrived at from different data.

On "Safety" specifically: EPICOR's own Safety category trends the same direction as Ignition's dramatic −94% interlock finding (down from 0.36 to 0.26 events/day) but on far fewer events (44 vs. 18) and isn't statistically significant on its own (p = 0.29) — EPICOR appears to log safety stops at a much coarser grain than Ignition's per-guard PLC signal. Directionally consistent, not an independent confirmation of the size of the win.

Two honest limits on this comparison: EPICOR's category totals (2,457 hrs across the full window) run well above Ignition's (1,361 hrs), and day-to-day correlation between the two systems on days both have data is weak (r = 0.15) — expected, since they define "downtime" differently (EPICOR's "Electrical" alone is a broad aggregate that likely rolls up many separate Ignition fault families) and clock/rounding differences shift events across midnight boundaries. Trend direction on the big categories is where the systems corroborate each other; day-level totals and exact hours are not directly comparable.

Trends & watch-outs (exposure-corrected)

Every comparison below uses an exact binomial test: given how a cause's total occurrences split between the baseline and recent periods, is the recent share different from what the recent period's share of total logged activity (19.6%) would predict on its own? Tested across 61 causes with at least 15 total occurrences, using a Bonferroni-corrected bar so "significant" here really means something.

The confirmed win. Guard/door interlock and stop-button stoppages fell from 8.49 to 0.49 per 24 logged-hours — a 94% drop that stays enormous even after the exposure correction (p = 7.8×10⁻⁶³). This is real. Worth documenting exactly what changed operationally around this so it can be repeated elsewhere.
